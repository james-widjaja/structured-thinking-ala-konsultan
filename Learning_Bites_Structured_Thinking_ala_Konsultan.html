Featured case study

Apollo 13: Thinking Under Pressure
Based on the 1995 film Apollo 13

The situation

In Ron Howard's Apollo 13, an oxygen-tank explosion cripples the spacecraft on the way to the Moon. Jim Lovell, Fred Haise, and Jack Swigert must abandon the mission and find a way home.

Movie reference: approx. 00:54-01:02, the explosion, the power-down, and Mission Control's first response. Timestamps can vary by release and playback version.

The leadership challenge

Gene Kranz and the flight controllers do not try to solve everything at once. They turn an overwhelming emergency into a sequence of specific questions, tests, decisions, and disciplined actions.

The goal changes from landing on the Moon to bringing the crew home alive.

What the film shows

Technical expertise matters, but structured thinking makes expertise usable under pressure: define the problem, make uncertainty visible, separate workstreams, and keep every action tied to the survival objective.

Facilitator prompt: At the moment the explosion happens, what should the team do first: search for a solution, or define the problem clearly?
Apollo 13 in the movie

Four thinking moves before action

1. Define the problem

Movie reference: approx. 00:55-01:02. The team moves beyond “we have a problem” to identify the life-threatening issue: the Service Module is damaged, power and oxygen are being lost, and the crew cannot complete the original mission.

Thinking move: name the real problem before naming a solution.

2. Separate facts from assumptions

Movie reference: approx. 01:03-01:15. Mission Control reads telemetry, listens to the crew, and checks what is actually known. The controllers do not assume the first explanation is complete; they test the evidence and update the picture.

Thinking move: label what is measured, what is reported, and what is still uncertain.

3. Break it into parts

Movie reference: approx. 01:16-01:32. Separate teams work on power, carbon dioxide, navigation, engine burns, communications, and the return trajectory while the astronauts manage immediate procedures.

Thinking move: decompose one crisis into manageable workstreams with clear owners.

4. Clarify the objective

Movie reference: approx. 01:10-01:20. Kranz makes the priority explicit: “Failure is not an option.” In practical terms, the objective is not a successful lunar landing; it is a safe return of the crew.

Thinking move: let the objective determine the trade-offs.

Facilitator prompt: Ask participants which facts changed the mission objective, and which assumptions had to be challenged.
Apollo 13 in the movie

Three thinking moves that turn plans into survival

5. Work within constraints

Movie reference: approx. 01:33-01:49. The engineers build the carbon-dioxide “mailbox” using only materials available on the spacecraft. The square Command Module filter must work with the round Lunar Module connection.

Thinking move: treat time, energy, materials, safety, and compatibility as design requirements.

6. Test solutions systematically

Movie reference: approx. 01:45-02:00. The ground team simulates the procedure, explains each step, and verifies the result before the crew executes it. The same disciplined approach is applied to navigation and the engine burns.

Thinking move: test the solution against reality before committing people to it.

7. Execute with discipline

Movie reference: approx. 02:00-02:17. Lovell, Haise, and Swigert follow checklists, ration power, perform the burn, and re-enter under pressure. The plan succeeds because execution stays precise when conditions are frightening.

Thinking move: communicate clearly, follow the agreed sequence, and monitor the result.

The structured-thinking lesson

Under pressure, do not jump from alarm to action. Move from problem to facts, parts, objective, constraints, tests, and then disciplined execution.

Movie reference: approx. 02:17-02:20, splashdown and recovery.

Facilitator prompt: Which of the seven moves would be easiest to skip in a real business crisis? What control would help your team keep it?

Habit 1

The Chairman's Question

An urgent request arrives. A number is needed tomorrow morning. People begin collecting data.

What is really being asked?

Clarify the Context

Before asking “what data do they need?”, ask “what decision is this supporting?”

  • What is the objective?
  • Who is making the decision?
  • What risks are being evaluated?
  • What recommendation would help?
Facilitator prompt: What should we clarify first? Let participants answer before introducing the decision and context.

What would you recommend to the MD?

Use the three habits

  • Clarify: What decision must leadership make?
  • Structure: How should we break the problem down?
  • Recommend: What action should we bring?

Prompt if needed: Who is impacted? Could this happen elsewhere? Why did controls fail? What should change system-wide?

Facilitator prompt: Let participants work through the thinking before revealing the three habits.
Final takeaway

Clarify → Structure → Recommend

1. Before I start

What is the context? What decision is being supported?

2. Before I analyze

How can I break this problem into clear parts?

3. Before I report

What recommendation, risk, and next step will I include?
